The commitment hold: does anyone charge the card — and what should ours say?

From the Aug 18 call: we're testing a credit-card screen after product selection, on roughly half of creators in the next 10–20 campaigns, used as a commitment device — never auto-charged. Part I checks whether anyone else in the industry does this and what the field does instead. Part II draws the screen and writes the copy — the part that decides whether this reads as StubHub-style process or as Benable fining its own creators.

Aug 18, 2026 Part I field research Part II screen + copy for Julia · Tony · Katie · Des
01

The straight answer on the credit-card trick

"Do other platforms also do this?"
No

No gifting or UGC platform stores a creator's card and charges it for not posting. If we run this, we're first in the category.

Across every platform checked — the gifting services, the commerce sample programs (TikTok Shop, Amazon Vine, Creator Connections, Walmart), and the paid UGC marketplaces (Insense, Billo, JoinBrands, Collabstr, Stack Influence) — zero store a payment card to charge on a no-post. Katie's instinct on the call was right: nobody in the industry does this. But three mechanisms get close, and they matter for how we frame ours:

① Insense's creator ToS is the nearest cousin (updated May 29, 2026): if a creator fails to deliver after "every effort" to solicit the content, the brand "reserves the right to charge the Creator for the product by sending an invoice," and the creator owes return-or-value. It's an after-the-fact invoice, not a stored card — and the medical no-show literature below suggests invoices like that mostly go unpaid.

② Stack Influence flips the payment order: creators buy the product with their own money and get reimbursed only after a verified post; no post, no reimbursement. Economically that's a 100% deposit — real creator money at risk, and their whole model runs on it (~$39/successful post to brands). TikTok Shop's "refundable samples" work the same way. ③ StubHub does charge the card on file — replacement-ticket cost plus a $25 fee when a seller fails to deliver — which is exactly the "process, not personal" feeling Tony cited; but that's a resale marketplace, not creator gifting.

So the honest positioning: the mechanism is proven, the placement is novel. Card-at-commitment is table stakes in restaurants, hotels, and ticket resale — it just hasn't been tried on gifted creator campaigns. Being first means the screen's copy carries the entire explanatory burden: there's no industry habit to lean on, only the restaurant-reservation mental model every creator already has.

02

What the field actually does about ghosting

seven mechanisms, from softest to hardest

Seven distinct mechanisms are in production across the industry, and they form a clean severity ladder. Every platform combines two or three — none uses a stored card. Benable today runs almost entirely on ⑦ (human chase), which is exactly why ghosting feels unsolved: it's the only rung with no structural consequence.

①

Pure relationship — no obligation by design

LTK · ShopMy — gifting marketed to brands as "no-obligation"; creators can even decline the gift.

The enforcement is invisible: delivery tracking + who-posted analytics tell the brand exactly who ghosted, and ghosts quietly stop getting gifted. Real-world post rate with zero enforcement: a skincare brand gifting 60 serums via ShopMy Lookbooks got ~25 pieces back (~40%).

Steal: nothing — this is the world Benable is trying to beat. ~40% is what "no stick at all" buys, and it's roughly Tony's ⅓-post-on-time number.

②

Gate future free product on past completion

TikTok Shop · Amazon Vine · Skeepers/Octoly — the platform-run sample programs, and the industry default.

TikTok Shop: post within 14 days of delivery (one 7-day self-serve extension), or your seller-visible fulfillment rate drops and sample access gets restricted or removed. Vine: review 90% of orders (60% program floor) or land in "Vine jail" → removal. Octoly: a missed deadline expires the product's credit; 3 expired credits = account disabled.

Steal: the hard day-count + the visible rate. This is the control arm's spine — "incomplete campaigns are what brands see when they pick creators."

③

Public reliability scores & badges

Billo · Collabstr · Fiverr · Upwork — reputation as the asset creators protect.

Billo computes an On-Time Delivery Score (% of last 3–20 videos delivered inside the 5-day window) and shows it to brands on every application. Fiverr tracks a 60-day Late Deliveries indicator and lets buyers review canceled orders; Upwork's JSS folds completion + timeline adherence into one number that decides who gets hired. Collabstr expirations "may negatively impact your profile ranking."

Steal: Tony's Upwork instinct, confirmed — but every one of these platforms pairs the score with a fairness valve (Billo excludes delays that weren't the creator's fault). This is Brian's v2 creator-memory work, already on the roadmap.

④

Escrow & forfeited earnings

Collabstr · Billo · JoinBrands · Amazon Creator Connections — paid-marketplace standard.

Brand money is held until delivery (Collabstr releases on approval or a 10-day silence timer); non-delivery refunds the brand and the creator forfeits the payout. Creator Connections adds strikes plus forfeited bonus commission. The creator's stake is money they would have earned — never money they already have.

Steal: nothing yet — it needs a payout to withhold. Becomes Benable-relevant the day paid campaigns ship (and it's the argument for holding bonuses until content is live).

⑤

Creator pays first, reimbursed on post

Stack Influence · JoinBrands reimbursement jobs · TikTok "refundable samples" — the only models with real creator money at risk.

The creator buys the product with their own money; a verified post triggers reimbursement (product + shipping + fees). No post, no reimbursement — Stack's help center says it plainly, and the brand is never charged. A de facto 100% deposit, and Stack's entire ~$39-per-successful-post business runs on it.

Steal: the existence proof. Creators demonstrably accept money-at-risk gifting at scale — the psychology of our card hold, with worse cash-flow for the creator than ours.

⑥

Invoice-the-ghost clauses

Insense creator ToS (May 2026) — the industry's hardest written policy.

After deadline + "every effort" to solicit content, the brand "reserves the right to charge the Creator for the product by sending an invoice," and the creator owes return-or-value. But it's paper: an invoice after the fact, with no stored payment method — and the no-show literature (§03) shows ~79% of after-the-fact fines simply go unpaid.

Steal: the ToS language pattern (ours needs an equivalent clause) — but the lesson is that without a card secured upfront, the clause is theater. Insense wrote the threat; we'd be the first to make it collectable.

⑦

Managed make-goods — absorb the risk

minisocial · Twirl · (Benable today) — the agency answer.

No public penalty touches the creator; the service eats it — content credits, re-runs with a new creator, human chasing. Nothing published passes cost to the creator; the brand just never sees the failure. This is Katie's blue-bubble text chain as a business model.

Steal: the brand-facing line ("we're still following up with the creators who haven't posted") is already right — the call confirmed it. What's missing is any structure underneath it, which is what ②, ③ and the card test add.

03

Why card-holds work — and when fines backfire

the behavioral evidence behind the copy choices

The card idea sits on real behavioral science — but the same science says a badly-worded version increases ghosting. Six findings, each with a direct consequence for the copy in Part II.

Works

Card-at-commitment cuts no-shows — the restaurant numbers

OpenTable's own data: a card hold makes diners up to 16% less likely to no-show and 15% less likely to cancel late; upfront deposits cut no-shows 57% on average. Tock's deposit-taking restaurants average a 1.7% no-show rate — "even a nominal $5 deposit" gets under 3%. One SevenRooms case: 15% → 1% with prepayment.

For us: secure the card at the moment of commitment (the accept flow), never later. The hold works at booking time; nothing works retroactively.
OpenTable payment-strategies + deposits pages · Tock resources · SevenRooms (vendor-published stats)
Backfires

"A fine is a price" — the daycare study

Ten Haifa daycares introduced a small (~$3) fine for late pickup. Late pickups roughly doubled — and stayed high after the fine was removed. The fine converted a moral obligation into a purchasable service: "worst case, it costs 10 NIS."

For us: a token charge ($20/week, Tony's floor idea) would let creators buy ghosting guilt-free. If a charge exists it must be the full product value, and the words "fine/fee/penalty" must never appear — they're the price-tag framing.
Gneezy & Rustichini, J. Legal Studies 2000 (verified from paper PDF)
Backfires

After-the-fact fines don't deter — and don't collect

A Danish RCT (6,746 patients) announced a €34 no-show fine: attendance didn't move, and 79% of the fines levied were never paid. A 61-study meta-analysis found fines "did not appear to reduce non-attendance" while small incentives did.

For us: this is why Insense's invoice clause is a paper tiger, and why "we'll invoice you" isn't a real alternative to the card. Either the card is secured upfront via Stripe, or the threat is theater.
BMC Health Services Research meta-analysis, Oct 2023 · Israel J Health Policy Research 2023
Framing

Restitution feels fair; commitment language deters

A field experiment on lateness fines found "covers our costs" framing deterred less than framing the act as breaking a commitment — but observers consistently rate restitution (make the harmed party whole) as fairer than punishment.

For us: use both halves — restitution for where the money goes ("covers the brand's $62 product cost") and commitment for why ("per the commitment you agreed to on Aug 25"). That's the StubHub voice: neutral process, buyer made whole, receipts not blame.
Kurz, Thomas & Fonseca, JESP 2014 · Theory and Decision 2019
Works

Deposit contracts work — when they're the default

Smokers offered a forfeit-on-failure deposit were 3.3–5.8pp more likely to quit — but only 11% opted in. Loss-framed deposits reliably beat equivalent rewards per participant; uptake is the weak link.

For us: the card step must be a default step in the flow for the test arm — presented as how it works, not an opt-in checkbox nobody would tick. (The escape hatch stays, quietly, ops-side.)
Giné, Karlan & Zinman, AEJ Applied 2010 · JMIR 2022 deposit RCTs
Framing

The industry's real stick is the permanent record

Airbnb fines flaking hosts (10–50% scaled, $50 min, $1,000 cap) and posts an unremovable automated review on their listing; Rover posts auto-records of late cancellations to sitter profiles; DoorDash deactivates below 90% completion. Fees come with documented-hardship waivers — the legitimacy valve.

For us: the control arm's consequence ("marked incomplete — that's what brands see") borrows this, and it's the consequence we keep forever even if the card test loses. Katie's manual waiver = our legitimacy valve.
Airbnb Host Cancellation Policy (article 990, verified) · Rover help center · DoorDash ratings pages

The Benable test, specced

what we decided · what to hold constant · what to measure
Decided on the Aug 18 call — the prototype below builds on these
  • Placement: after product selection — we already have everything we need from them
  • Split: ~half of creators across the next 10–20 campaigns see the card step
  • Threat only: nothing automated ever pulls money; any charge would be a manual, human call
  • Escape hatch: stall at the card step → Katie emails "we've removed that step for you," order stands
  • Carve-out: proven repeat creators shouldn't meet the hammer (Katie), relax it wherever needed (Tony)
  • Alongside: stricter "school-teacher" messaging for everyone — the card is only one arm of the test

The fairness spec — when a charge could ever fire

  • Product marked delivered (courier-confirmed, not shipped) and the campaign has closed
  • Nothing posted and no draft submitted — a late draft in review is never chargeable
  • No reply on any channel across at least 3 touches on 2+ channels (text, email, DM)
  • An extension was offered in writing and ignored — one tap, no reason needed
  • Katie approves manually. No cron job ever charges a card
  • Amount = product retail value only, charged once, receipt sent immediately
  • Post-and-refund: if they post within 7 days of the charge, refund it in full. The goal was always the content, and it turns the charge into a deposit, not a fine
This list is the product. If we can't honestly print every line of it on the screen, we shouldn't collect the card.

Measure — the test is two funnels, not one

  • Card arm: product-pick → card-screen completion rate; stall rate; back-out rate after seeing it
  • Both arms: delivered → posted-on-time rate, delivered → posted-ever rate, days-late median
  • Quality guard: accept rate on the next invite for creators who saw the card screen
  • Ops load: Katie escalation touches per campaign, before vs. after
  • Kill criteria upfront: if card-screen stalls > ~25% and post-rate lift < ~10pts, the hold loses to messaging-only
Half the value of the test is honest control-arm copy: the no-card cohort gets the same stricter ladder, minus the hold — so we learn whether words alone close the gap.
Two compliance notes. ① Automated escalation texts only go to creators who ticked the SMS box in the portal (the TCPA opt-in) — everyone else gets the same copy from Katie's phone, manually. ② Charging a stored card requires the cardholder agreement language on-screen at collection time (the fine-print line under the CTA) — Stripe's SetupIntent flow covers this, but the words must actually appear.

All three sit in the same slot: the creator just accepted, picked their products, and confirmed their address — the happiest beat of the flow, which is exactly why the words matter. Every variant must answer three questions within one screen-glance: am I paying money now (no), when exactly would I ever be charged (spelled out), and who's in control of that (you are — post or just reply, and it never happens).

Ladder ruleswhy each rung is shaped this way
ABoth arms escalate identically. Same days, same structure — only the named consequence differs (hold vs. standing). Otherwise the test measures copy, not the card.
1The extension always comes before the consequence — in the same message, earlier in the sentence. The ladder's job is replies, not charges; a charge is the ladder failing.
2Katie appears at +2, the process voice at +5. Human warmth in the middle rung ("the brand's asking me for a date" — she's on your side, managing up), institutional voice for the final notice. Good cop is a person; bad cop is a system.
3Dates, not day-counts. "Closes Mon, Sep 14" is checkable and calm; "5 days!!" is a nag. Every consequence has a date, every date has an exit.
4"Posting or simply replying stops this — always" closes every hard message. It's the pressure-release valve that makes the strictness feel fair, and it's literally true per the fairness spec.
5Nothing sends on holidays/weekends — rungs shift to the next weekday morning. Being berated on Christmas Day is how a policy becomes a screenshot.

*"Deposit" implies we're holding their money now — factually wrong for a $0 hold. The daycare study is the deeper reason to avoid fine-words: a named "fine" becomes a price, and a price makes ghosting purchasable.

Words that do the work

  • hold
  • commitment
  • covers the brand's product cost
  • expires when you post
  • $0 today
  • one tap to extend
  • no reason needed
  • replying stops this — always

Restitution grammar throughout: the money, if it ever moves, goes to make the brand whole — Benable never profits from a charge. Say that anywhere trust wobbles.
